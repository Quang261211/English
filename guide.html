Deep dive: understand the tense, not just the keyword

A reliable way to choose a tense is to identify the reference time first, then decide whether you are describing a fact, an action in progress, a completed result, or the duration of an activity.

01 · SIMPLE

Facts, habits & whole events

Use a simple form to state a routine, fact, or event as a whole. It does not focus on the action's progress.

She plays tennis every Sunday. · We visited Hue last summer.
02 · CONTINUOUS

In progress around a time

Use be + V-ing to put the reader inside an ongoing or temporary action at a reference time.

She is studying now. · At 8 p.m., she was studying.
03 · PERFECT

Earlier than a reference point

Use have + V3 to connect an earlier action to a reference time: now, a past moment, or a future deadline.

I have finished. · They had left before we arrived.
04 · PERFECT CONTINUOUS

Duration up to a reference point

Use have been + V-ing to emphasize how long an activity has continued up to now, a past point, or a future point.

He has been reading for an hour. · By June, he will have been working here for a year.

Build questions and negatives

  1. Find the first auxiliary verb: be, have, will or a form of do.
  2. For a negative, place not after the first auxiliary: has not finished.
  3. For a question, move the first auxiliary before the subject: Have you finished?
  4. If Present Simple or Past Simple has no auxiliary, add do / does / did: Does she work? / Did they go?

Position of common time expressions

  • Already / just / ever / never often appear between the auxiliary and V3: has already left.
  • Yet commonly appears at the end of questions and negatives: Have you called yet?
  • Since + starting point; for + duration: since Monday / for three days.
  • By introduces a deadline or reference point: by Friday, by the time we arrive.

Three traps to avoid

  • Finished past time: use Past Simple with yesterday, last year, in 2020; do not normally pair these with Present Perfect.
  • Stative verbs: verbs such as know, believe, own, need are not usually continuous when they describe a state.
  • Third-person -s: in Present Simple affirmative sentences, add -s / -es with he, she, it; after does, return to V1.

Future time clauses

In time clauses introduced by when, before, after, as soon as and until, English commonly uses a present form for a future meaning.

Correct: I'll call you when I arrive.
Not usually: I'll call you when I will arrive.
Important: Signal words are clues, not automatic answers. For example, for can appear with several tenses. Read the whole sentence and ask what the speaker wants to emphasize.

Easy comparisons

Some tenses look similar. Focus on what the speaker wants to emphasize.

Present Simple vs Present Continuous

Present Simple = habits, facts, routines.
Present Continuous = something happening now or temporarily.

“I study English every day.” / “I am studying English now.”

Past Simple vs Present Perfect

Past Simple = a finished past time, often stated.
Present Perfect = past action connected to now; the exact finished time is not the focus.

“I visited Hue in 2024.” / “I have visited Hue.”

Present Perfect vs Present Perfect Continuous

Present Perfect often emphasizes a result or experience. Present Perfect Continuous emphasizes the activity or its duration.

“I have read three chapters.” / “I have been reading for two hours.”

Will vs Be going to

Will often expresses a spontaneous decision, promise, or prediction. Be going to often expresses an existing plan or a prediction based on present evidence.

“I'll answer the phone.” / “Look at those clouds. It's going to rain.”

Study note: The 12-tense framework is a common teaching model; “be going to” is included here as the 13th, near-future structure for learning convenience. Signal words are clues, not automatic rules.
